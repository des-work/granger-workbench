# Nordic  (design profile: `nordic`)

**Era:** early 20th century; golden age 1950s-60s; continuous since

*Long winter light handled well: muted blue-greys, pale wood, generous space and nothing shouting. Calm without being cold, plain without being cheap — designed for a room that is dark for most of the day.*

## Origins

Scandinavian design, which emerged in the early twentieth century and flourished through the 1950s across Denmark, Finland, Norway, Sweden and Iceland on a programme of simplicity, minimalism and functionality. Aalto in architecture and furniture, Jacobsen and Wegner in chairs, Poul Henningsen in lamps, Maija Isola in textiles; the Lunning Prize from 1951 and the Brooklyn Museum's 1954 'Design in Scandinavia' exported it. Its stated ideal was democratic: good objects at a price ordinary people pay.

## Composition & space

Calm and horizontal, with real breathing room and furniture-like proportions. Nothing is crowded and nothing is centred for ceremony; elements sit where they are useful. Negative space is treated as material, in the way an empty wall is part of a Nordic room.

## Proportion & measure

A restrained 1.25 scale over four steps — this style does not raise its voice typographically either. Measure 68ch, gutters 24px, radius 10px: softened but not rounded. Components are generously padded, because cramped is the one thing this aesthetic cannot be.

## Color logic

The existing nordic-frost palette is the polar-night application mode, not a definition of Scandinavian design. The nordic-daylight page uses frost, slate ink, pale timber and quiet blue. Warm material and useful proportions matter more than desaturation; the movement also includes vivid textiles.

## Light

Low, soft and lateral: the light of a sun that stays near the horizon. Shadows are long, diffuse and gentle; there is no overhead glare. The reference object is a Henningsen lamp — layered shades that give light with no visible bulb and no hard edge anywhere.

## Typography

A humanist grotesque with open shapes (Inter) at a comfortable size, with a slightly geometric display face (Sora) above. Set loose, never tight, and never fewer than the ascenders need. Hierarchy is quiet — a step in size and a step in colour, rarely weight.

## Materiality & finish

Pale ash and birch, wool and linen, matte ceramic, brushed steel, unlacquered leather. Materials are honest and age visibly; nothing is coated to look like something else. A surface should look like it would be pleasant to touch.

## Motion & rhythm

Gentle and brief — 220ms on a soft ease, small travel. Motion behaves like a well-made drawer: damped, quiet, arriving without a bounce. Nothing springs, because nothing in a Nordic interior springs.

## Imagery

Landscape and interior photography in flat northern light, lots of sky and snow, objects shot plainly against pale backgrounds. People are shown using things rather than posing with them. Illustration is thin-line and sparse.

## Voice (copy & tone)

Plain, warm and unhurried; short sentences, no superlatives, no urgency. The tone assumes the reader is an adult who will decide in their own time — a style that would rather be trusted than clicked.

## Sound

Wind, water, wood, a fire somewhere else in the house. Very quiet interface sounds with soft attacks — nothing percussive and nothing bright. Silence is comfortable here rather than empty.

## Where it lives

Furniture and homeware, wellness and healthcare, fintech that wants to feel safe, public services in the Nordics, productivity software with a calm posture, and sustainability brands that mean it.

## Kinship (family tree)

Shares `neutral`'s restraint but adds warmth and a cooler palette; shares `earthy`'s honesty about materials with the colour temperature reversed. `organic-tech` is this style with the curves turned up. Don't confuse with minimalism for its own sake: Scandinavian design is about comfort and affordability, not austerity.

## Hierarchy of power

Space > light > material honesty > typography > the one cool accent. If the layout doesn't feel like a room you could stand in comfortably, adjusting the palette won't help.

## Canon (study these)

Study: Aalto's Paimio chair and Savoy vase, Henningsen's PH lamps, Wegner's Wishbone, Marimekko's Isola prints, the 1954 'Design in Scandinavia' catalogue, and Nordic public-service design systems.

## Adaptation (modes & accessibility)

Snow on polar night runs 11:1 with the muted grey holding 5:1. The light inversion — frost field, slate ink — is equally canonical and arguably the summer version of the same room. Reduced motion costs little, since the motion was already small and damped.

## Signature moves

1. Use a horizontal object catalogue with generous practical space
2. Stage pale timber, matte ceramic and a layered lamp in lateral daylight
3. Use quiet humanist typography and functional labels
4. Keep polar-night colours for established applications; use the named daylight mode for interiors

## Components (this vault)

- **tokens/style**: `nordic` (`assets kit nordic` for a full stylesheet)
- **palette**: `nordic-frost` — #2e3440 #3b4252 #4c566a #88c0d0 #a3be8c #d8dee9 #eceff4
- **fonts**: display: Sora, body: Inter
- **textures (native)**: grain, paper
- **gloss**: very low — matte ceramic and oiled wood, with a single soft sheen where a glass or steel object catches the low sun. A high-gloss surface would read as plastic, which is the one material this tradition is suspicious of.
- **shapes**: pebble-stack, leaf-vein, grid-marks
- **patterns**: dots, topographic
- **techniques (fx)**: layered-shadows, squircle-corners, scroll-reveal, light-dark-fn

**Rules:** DO give everything room, desaturate toward grey, and keep light soft and lateral. DON'T saturate, crowd, or add a second accent; and don't mistake plainness for the goal — the goal is comfort.

**You've lost the plot when:** Fails by turning cold — remove the warmth and it becomes `neutral` in a cardigan. Fails also as marketing shorthand: pale wood and a sans-serif is not the tradition, which was fundamentally about well-made objects being affordable.
