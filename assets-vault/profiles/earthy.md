# Earthy  (design profile: `earthy`)

**Era:** palette of the 1970s-early 80s; revived through craft from c. 2015

*Clay, rust, olive and sand: a palette taken from fired pottery and dry landscape, set in a warm serif with plenty of room. Grounded and unhurried, with the handmade left visible rather than sanded off.*

## Origins

Earth tones — browns, greens, greys and warm muted shades drawn from natural materials — were the palette of the 1970s and early 1980s, rising with the environmental movement as people reached for something more natural than what came before. Memphis neon displaced them in the mid-to-late eighties; they returned through craft, ceramics and sustainability branding, which now use them to signal authenticity and environmental seriousness.

## Composition & space

Relaxed and asymmetric, with organic spacing rather than a visible mathematical grid. Elements settle rather than align — a layout that feels arranged by hand. Generous margins, soft section breaks, and room for an image to sit slightly off-axis without it reading as a mistake.

## Proportion & measure

A 1.25 scale over four steps at a 17px base, 66ch measure, 8px radius. Nothing is tight: padding runs loose and line spacing comfortable, because the whole posture is unhurried. Rules are soft-edged or omitted in favour of space.

## Color logic

Terracotta and rust against sand and bone, with olive and moss as the cool counterweight. Everything is muted — these are pigments mixed with grey and ochre, not hues picked off a wheel. Nothing here is fully saturated, and nothing is fluorescent.

## Light

Warm, low, late-afternoon: light that has travelled through dust. Shadows are soft and brown rather than grey, and the whole image sits slightly warm. The reference is a window in a room with a tiled floor at five o'clock.

## Typography

A warm old-style serif for reading (Lora) with a characterful display cut above (Fraunces), set loose. Real italics, oldstyle figures, and a willingness to let a heading have a little personality — the type should feel like it was chosen rather than specified.

## Materiality & finish

Unglazed and matte-glazed ceramic, raw linen, jute, oiled wood, hammered copper, lime plaster. Every surface has tooth; variation is a sign of the hand rather than a defect, and nothing has been polished to uniformity.

## Motion & rhythm

Slow and soft — 300ms with an easing that settles rather than snaps. Things fade and drift a short distance. Nothing springs or bounces, because nothing made of clay does.

## Imagery

Still life with pottery and raw ingredients, hands working, dry landscape, textiles photographed close enough to see the weave. Natural light only. The camera lingers on texture and imperfection rather than hiding them.

## Voice (copy & tone)

Warm, direct and a little plainspoken; first person is welcome. Talks about process and provenance — where it came from, who made it, how long it took — without slipping into sanctimony about it.

## Sound

Room tone with birds or a kettle, wood and ceramic contact sounds, acoustic instruments. Interface sounds are soft and woody, with no digital brightness anywhere in them.

## Where it lives

Food and beverage, ceramics and craft retail, wellness, sustainability and agriculture brands, slow-travel and hospitality, cookbooks — anywhere provenance is part of the product.

## Kinship (family tree)

Shares `nordic`'s material honesty at the opposite colour temperature, and `organic-tech`'s curves without the technology. Cousin of `dark-academia` through the serif and the patina, though academia is interior and nocturnal while this is outdoors at four in the afternoon. Don't confuse with rustic pastiche: the palette is muted, not dirty.

## Hierarchy of power

Palette warmth > material texture > typography > generous space > imagery. Get the muting wrong — let one hue go saturated — and the whole thing turns into autumn clip-art.

## Canon (study these)

Study: 1970s environmental-movement graphics, Bauhaus and mid-century studio pottery, Leach and Hamada's ceramics, Kinfolk's photography, and the branding of any serious coffee roaster.

## Adaptation (modes & accessibility)

Espresso on sand runs 9:1, though the muted secondary sits near 3.6:1 and should be darkened before it carries text. The dark inversion — bone type on deep clay — works and keeps the warmth, which matters, since a cold dark mode would break the whole premise.

## Signature moves

1. Mute every hue with grey or ochre before using it
2. Set a warm serif loose, with real italics doing work
3. Let an image sit slightly off-axis and leave it there
4. Keep the texture visible at the scale it is actually seen

- **gloss**: almost none — the sheen of a glazed rim on an otherwise matte pot. Where gloss appears it is partial and uneven, because a kiln does not produce a uniform finish and pretending otherwise loses the handmade claim.

- **patterns**: noise-texture, topographic

**Rules:** DO mute every hue toward ochre or grey, keep light warm and low, and leave texture visible. DON'T saturate, don't use a cool grey, and don't sand the imperfection out — uniformity is what this style is reacting against.

**You've lost the plot when:** Fails by saturation — one pure orange and the clay becomes Halloween. Fails equally by over-polish: the appeal rests on visible making, so a perfectly uniform surface reads as a stock photo of craft rather than the thing itself.
